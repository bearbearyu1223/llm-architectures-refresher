1. What gets quantized, and what rounding costs         [post §1]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  blocks (layers)                    24
  d_model (vector per token)         896
  attention heads / KV heads         14 / 2
  MLP inner width                    4864
  vocabulary                         151,936
  parameters                         494.0M

  'Quantize the model' means the projection matrices and nothing else.
  'tensors' counts stored arrays, not layers: 4 attention matrices in
  each of 24 blocks is 96, and 3 MLP matrices in each is 72.

  part                   tensors  params  share  quantized
  ----------------------------------------------------------
  attention q,k,v,o           96   44.0M   8.9%        yes
  MLP gate,up,down            72  313.8M  63.5%        yes
  embedding (= lm_head)        1  136.1M  27.6%         no
  norms and biases           121    0.1M   0.0%         no

  The MLP carries most of it. Note also that k_proj and v_proj are
  7x smaller than q_proj: this model uses grouped-query attention,
  so several query heads share one set of keys and values (post 2).

  One real weight matrix: layer 11 down_proj, (896, 4864), fp32.

  absmax |w|                         0.6133
  std of w                           0.0183
  absmax / std                       33.6

  INT8 spreads 255 levels evenly across [-absmax, +absmax], so one step is
  absmax/127 and nothing can be represented more finely than half of that:

  step = absmax / 127                4.829e-03
  worst rounding error = step/2      2.414e-03
  measured max |q(w) - w|            2.414e-03

  Those last two agree, which is the check: the error is not mysterious,
  it is half a grid step, and the grid step is set by the largest weight
  sharing the grid. Everything else in this post follows from that.

  >> Quantization error is half a grid step, and the step is fixed by the
     biggest element on the grid. Shrink what shares a grid and you shrink
     the error for everything on it.

2. Where the outliers actually live                    [post §2]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Spread across channels, as a multiple of the median channel's max.
  Weights first — one row per output channel:

  tensor                max / median  channels > 5x  channels
  -------------------------------------------------------------
  L0 down_proj.weight           4.2x              0       896
  L5 down_proj.weight           7.6x              3       896
  L11 down_proj.weight          7.0x              4       896
  L17 down_proj.weight          4.6x              0       896
  L23 down_proj.weight          7.3x              1       896

  Now the activations flowing into those same layers:

  tensor         max / median  channels > 5x  channels
  ------------------------------------------------------
  L0 o_proj              7.9x              5       896
  L0 down_proj          11.7x            155      4864
  L5 o_proj              3.8x              0       896
  L5 down_proj          85.5x            224      4864
  L11 o_proj             5.4x              2       896
  L11 down_proj         10.3x             28      4864
  L17 o_proj             5.5x              2       896
  L17 down_proj         20.9x            158      4864
  L23 o_proj             8.2x             22       896
  L23 down_proj         19.1x             97      4864

  Weights sit within a single order of magnitude of each other. The
  activations into down_proj do not. A grid sized to hold a channel 85x
  above the median has to be wide enough that ordinary values land on
  almost the same level.

  >> Outliers are an activation phenomenon, not a weight phenomenon. That
     single fact is why production quantization is almost always weight-
     only.

3. Are they the same channels every time?              [post §3]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Top-10 outlier channels at layer 5, across five unrelated inputs
  (English prose, science, economics, Python source, Spanish):

  input pair                  shared of top-10
  ----------------------------------------------
  English prose vs science                3/10
  science vs economics                    2/10
  economics vs Python source              2/10
  Python source vs Spanish                2/10

  shared by all five inputs          2/10
  distinct channels across inputs    41

  shared by all 6 sampled layers     0/10

  So the textbook line — that the same few dimensions are extreme
  everywhere — is only partly true here. A small core does persist: two
  channels are in the top ten for all five inputs, including Python source
  and Spanish, which is not a coincidence you get for free. But most of
  the outliers move with the input, and across layers there is no overlap
  at all. The systematic version of this is documented at 6.7B and above
  (Dettmers et al.); this is a 0.5B model and not measured at that scale.

  >> Enough of the outlier structure is stable to matter, and not enough
     to let you pick the channels once and hard-code them. Either way the
     fix is the same: never let a whole tensor share one scale.

4. Same bits, different scale placement                [post §4]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Identical format — 8 bits, evenly spaced. Only the scope of the scale
  changes: one divisor for the whole tensor, or one per row.


  What each scale has to cover, for L11 down_proj:

  weights per scale, per-tensor      4,358,144
  weights per scale, per-row         4,864

  tensor absmax                      0.6133
  row absmax, median                 0.0879
  rows under half the tensor absmax  881 of 896

  tensor                      per-tensor  per-row  ratio
  --------------------------------------------------------
  L0 down_proj.weight              3.81%    1.14%   3.3x
  L11 down_proj.weight             7.63%    1.41%   5.4x
  L23 down_proj.weight             6.69%    1.14%   5.9x
  L5 down_proj (activations)      58.10%    6.10%   9.5x

  For weights, splitting the scale by row is worth a few times less
  error - worth having, not decisive. For the activation tensor it is
  the difference between a usable number and a destroyed one, and the
  reason is the row above: that tensor has a channel two orders of
  magnitude above its median, and per-tensor scaling makes every other
  channel share a grid built to survive it.

  >> Same bits, same spacing, two orders of magnitude of difference in
     error. What matters is not how many bits you keep but how much
     dynamic range is forced to share one scale.

5. NF4, derived rather than copied                     [post §5]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  NF4's 16 levels are the equal-probability quantiles of a standard
  normal, not a hand-picked table. Deriving them and comparing against
  the constants bitsandbytes ships:

  levels derived                     16
  max |derived - bitsandbytes|       5.96e-08

  narrowest step (near zero)         0.0796
  widest step (near +/-1)            0.3038
  ratio                              3.82x

  That ratio is the whole idea. Weights are roughly normal, so most of
  them are near zero — and NF4 puts its levels closest together exactly
  there, spending resolution where the values are instead of spreading it
  evenly over a range that is mostly empty.

  Both formats on the same matrix, at their real stored cost:

  format                  rel RMSE  bits/weight incl. scales
  ------------------------------------------------------------
  INT8 per-tensor            7.63%                     8.000
  INT8 per-channel           1.41%                     8.007
  INT4 uniform, block=64    12.21%                     4.500
  NF4 block=64               9.64%                     4.500
  NF4 block=256             11.15%                     4.125

  The comparison that tests NF4's claim is the middle pair: uniform INT4
  against NF4 at the same width and the same number of scales, so the
  only variable left is where the levels sit. NF4 cuts the error by
  about a fifth, which is what shaping the grid to the data buys.

  It does *not* beat 8-bit, and no rearrangement of 16 levels was ever
  going to beat 256 of them. What it does is get within a few points of
  per-tensor INT8 at half the storage — and per-channel INT8, at 1.4%,
  is better than both by a wide margin.

  Note the bits column: NF4 at block=64 carries an fp32 scale for every
  64 weights, half a bit on top of four, so a '4-bit' model is really a
  4.5-bit one. Widening to 256 buys that down to 4.125 and pays for it in
  error, because more weights then share one absmax.

  >> At equal width, placing the levels where the weights are beats
     spacing them evenly by about a quarter of the error. Against 8 bits
     it loses on error and wins on size, which is the trade actually on
     offer.

6. What it actually saves                              [post §6]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  lm_head.weight is embed_tokens.weight  yes

  parameters                         494.0M
  of which embedding (tied)          136.1M  (28%)
  of which other Linear              357.8M  (72%)

  Model size if the Linear weights are quantized and everything else —
  embeddings, norms — is left in fp16, which is what real tools do:

  scheme            bits/weight (Linear)  model size  vs fp16
  -------------------------------------------------------------
  fp16 (baseline)                 16.000     942 MiB    1.00x
  INT8 per-channel                 8.036     603 MiB    1.56x
  NF4 block=64                     4.500     452 MiB    2.09x

  The shrink is real but smaller than the bit-width suggests, because a
  quarter of this model is an embedding table nobody quantizes and the
  scales ride along with every block.

  >> 4-bit weights do not make a 4x smaller model. Scales, and the tensors
     left alone on purpose, both dilute the headline number.

7. Perplexity against the tail                         [post §7]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Reference: unquantized fp32. Eval passage is 550 tokens of
  non-repeating prose, shipped in the demo so the number reproduces.

  scheme                perplexity  vs fp32   mean KL    max KL  max/mean
  -------------------------------------------------------------------------
  fp32 reference            20.869        —         —         —         —
  INT8 per-channel          20.913   +0.21%  2.60e-03  1.76e-01       68x
  NF4 block=64              24.104  +15.50%  1.57e-01  1.09e+01       69x
  NF4 block=64, + head      26.486  +26.92%  2.44e-01  1.09e+01       45x

  Read the INT8 row twice. Perplexity moves by a fraction of a percent —
  a number that clears any ship/no-ship gate anyone sets. The same run
  has a token whose predicted distribution moved by tens of times the
  average. Perplexity is a mean over thousands of tokens, and a mean is
  exactly the statistic that cannot see a tail.

  INT8 KL at quantile 0.5            1.730e-03
  INT8 KL at quantile 0.9            4.341e-03
  INT8 KL at quantile 0.99           1.194e-02
  INT8 KL at quantile 0.999          9.441e-02
  INT8 KL at quantile 1.0            1.756e-01

  >> Perplexity says INT8 weight-only is free. The tail of the same
     distribution says a handful of tokens changed a great deal. Both are
     true, and only one of them is what a user meets.
